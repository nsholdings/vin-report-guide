Curbstoning: when a "private seller" is really an unlicensed dealer

Last checked September 20, 2026

Curbstoning is the practice of selling cars as a business while posing as a private individual. The name comes from selling cars parked at the curb. The point is to avoid the obligations a licensed dealer carries — and those obligations exist to protect you.

What you lose when the seller isn't who they say

  • The Buyers Guide. The FTC Used Car Rule (16 CFR Part 455) requires dealers — defined as people who have sold or offered five or more used vehicles in the previous twelve months — to disclose warranty terms on a posted Buyers Guide. A curbstoner is a dealer by that definition but posts nothing. See the dealer Buyers Guide.
  • A licensed business you can find later. State dealer licensing typically comes with an address, a bond, and a regulator to complain to.
  • An honest history. Curbstoners frequently deal in cars they bought cheaply for a reason — salvage, flood, high mileage — and present them as a family car.

Signs you are dealing with one

  • The seller's name is not on the title — they are "selling it for a friend" or "for a relative". See title jumping.
  • The same phone number appears on several different car listings
  • They cannot answer basic ownership questions: where it was serviced, how long they have had it, why they are selling
  • They want to meet in a parking lot rather than at their home
  • The car was registered to them only very recently
Search the phone numberPaste the seller's number into a search engine and into the listing site's search. Finding it on several other cars being sold "privately" is one of the quickest signals there is.

Check the record

A history report will show how long the current owner has had the car, and whether it moved through an auction recently. A "family car" registered to the seller three weeks ago after passing through a salvage auction is a very different proposition from what you were told.

The record shows how long the seller has actually owned the car — and what it was before they had it.

What to do

If the title is not in the seller's name, do not buy the car. If you believe someone is operating as an unlicensed dealer, your state's dealer licensing agency or attorney general's office is who to tell.
