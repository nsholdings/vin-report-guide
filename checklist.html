The pre-purchase checklist

The order matters. Each step is cheaper than the one after it, so do them in sequence and stop as soon as something fails.

Step 1 — Get the VIN yourself (free, 2 minutes)

  • Read the 17 characters off the dashboard through the windshield
  • Read them again off the door-jamb sticker
  • Read them off the title and the registration
  • Confirm all four match, character by character

If the seller resists any part of this, stop here.

Step 2 — Run the free checks (free, 5 minutes)

  • NHTSA for open safety recalls
  • NICB VINCheck for theft, salvage and flood reports — remember the five-lookup daily limit, so use them on cars you are serious about

An open recall is not automatically a dealbreaker — recall repairs are performed free of charge by the manufacturer's dealers. A theft or salvage report is a different matter.

Step 3 — Pull a full history report

This is where you find the things the free checks cannot see: title brands from other states, the odometer sequence, accident and auction records, liens, and prior commercial use.

Pull the full record before you book an inspection. It is the cheaper of the two checks, and it disqualifies more cars.

Step 4 — Read the report against the seller's story

You are not looking for a clean report. You are looking for consistency. Ask directly about anything that does not line up:

  • A gap of several years with no recorded activity
  • Registration in a state the seller has never mentioned
  • An accident record the seller described as "a scratch"
  • An odometer reading out of sequence
  • A lien with no release on file

A straight answer to an awkward question is a good sign. A vague one is information too.

Step 5 — Pay for a pre-purchase inspection

An independent mechanic, chosen by you, not by the seller. This is the most expensive check, which is why it is last. By this point you are only paying to inspect a car whose paperwork already holds up.

Step 6 — Then negotiate

Everything you found in steps 2 through 5 is now a documented fact rather than a suspicion, and documented facts are what move a price.

One rule that survives every version of this listNever send a deposit for a vehicle you have not seen, to a seller you cannot verify, on the strength of a VIN they gave you. Insist on reading the VIN off the car yourself.
