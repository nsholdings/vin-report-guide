Vehicle history reports: what the different types actually contain

Last checked September 13, 2026

"Vehicle history report" describes at least three different products built from different data. Knowing which one you are buying matters, because they fail in different places.

1. Federal title data (NMVTIS)

The National Motor Vehicle Title Information System is operated by the Bureau of Justice Assistance, part of the US Department of Justice. It exists to protect consumers from fraud and unsafe vehicles and to stop stolen vehicles from being resold. Its core is title data reported by state motor vehicle agencies, plus data from junk and salvage yards and insurance carriers.

Strength: title brands across states. This is the data that catches title washing, because it is not limited to whichever state the car currently sits in.

Limit: it is a title and salvage database. It is not built to tell you about a fender bender that never reached a title.

Access: consumers buy through approved providers; the government site links out to them. Source: NMVTIS (US Dept. of Justice).

2. Commercial history reports

These aggregate title data with other feeds: insurance claims, police-reported accidents, service and repair records from participating shops, auction listings, emissions and inspection records, past sale listings.

Strength: breadth. Service entries and auction records are where a car's real life shows up, and where a story stops matching the paperwork.

Limit: coverage depends on who reports to them. A repair paid in cash at a shop that reports to nobody leaves no trace anywhere, in any report, at any price.

3. Free single-purpose lookups

NHTSA Recalls returns open safety recalls only — not recalls already repaired, not recalls more than 15 years old (except where a manufacturer offers longer coverage), not small or specialty manufacturers, not international vehicles.

NICB VINCheck returns theft, salvage and flood reports drawn from participating insurers, capped at five searches per 24 hours per IP address. Only insurance records appear — police records do not.

Strength: free, instant, authoritative within their scope.

Limit: each answers one question. Neither gives you an odometer sequence, a lien, or a title history.

No report is completeEvery one of these is a record of what someone chose to report. A clean report means "no adverse event was recorded", not "nothing happened". Treat it as a filter, not a guarantee, and still pay for an inspection.
