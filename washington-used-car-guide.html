Buying a used car in Washington: the WA REBUILT brand

Last checked November 1, 2026

Washington's rules start with a question most states skip: who reported the car. The answer changes the label, and the label is the first thing you should ask about.

Total loss or destroyed

Under Washington's rule, a vehicle is a total loss when an insurer reports its destruction to the department, and a destroyed vehicle when the owner reports it (WAC 308-56A-460). Insurers can report electronically, by submitting a title or affidavit marked DESTROYED, or with a total loss claim settlement form, and every report must carry the date of loss.

The date of loss is more useful to a buyer than it looks. It anchors the damage to a point in time, which you can line up against the car's service and registration record.

The six-to-twenty-year rule

For vehicles between six and twenty years old, the report must also state whether the vehicle meets the market value threshold. That extra question exists because an older car's repair bill reaches its value far sooner, and Washington does not want every middle-aged fender bender producing a brand.

For you this means the age of the car at the date of loss changes how much the record means. A three-year-old car reported destroyed is a serious event. A fifteen-year-old car may have been reported for a far smaller one.

The date of loss and the reporting party are title-record details. A full history report is where they surface.

Getting back on the road, and the brand that follows

To retitle a rebuilt vehicle, the rebuilder submits an application for a certificate of title under RCW 46.12.530, a certificate of vehicle inspection, a bill of sale from whoever reported the destruction — insurer, owner or wrecker — and an odometer disclosure statement where applicable. Insurer bills of sale need the representative's signature and title; owner bills of sale must be notarised or certified.

Dealers face an extra step: before selling a destroyed or wrecked vehicle, a dealer must rebuild it to state or federal safety standards, obtain a Washington State Patrol inspection, and hold a certificate of ownership in the dealer's own name.

Vehicles meeting the criteria are then branded WA REBUILT on the certificate of ownership or registration certificate. That is the exact phrase to look for.

Ask for the inspection certificate, not just the titleThe brand tells you the car passed. The certificate tells you when, and by whom. A rebuilt car whose owner cannot produce it is asking you to take the paperwork on trust.

What to check on a Washington car

  1. Ask whether the record shows a total loss (insurer-reported) or destroyed (owner-reported), and get the date of loss.
  2. Work out the car's age at that date, then ask how the market value threshold was answered.
  3. For a rebuilt car, ask for the State Patrol inspection certificate and the bill of sale from the reporting party.
  4. If you are bringing the car in from out of state, read buying a used car in another state first — brands do not always travel intact.
  5. Inspect independently. See the pre-purchase inspection.

And treat an unbranded Washington car with visible repair work as a question rather than an answer — see why a clean title does not mean no accidents.
