How to read a vehicle history report

Last checked September 13, 2026

Most people scan a report for the word "accident", see nothing, and stop. The useful information is in the sequence and the gaps.

Title records

What to read: how many title transfers, which states, and in what order.

Normal looks like a handful of transfers over a decade, mostly in one or two states. What deserves a question is a cluster of transfers in a short window across several states — that is the shape title washing leaves behind, because a brand applied in one state can be lost when a car is retitled in another with different rules.

Also read the current brand status and the historical entries. A currently clean title does not mean it was always clean.

Odometer readings

What to read: the numbers in date order.

Readings are captured at title transfers, inspections and service visits. You are checking that the sequence only ever increases, and that the rate of increase is plausible. A car that gained 4,000 miles in three years and then 60,000 in one is telling you something — either about its use or about its records.

A single reading proves nothing at all. The sequence is the evidence.

Accident and damage entries

What to read: the date, the reported severity, and whether airbags deployed.

An entry does not mean the car is bad. Absence of an entry does not mean it was never hit — an accident paid for in cash, at a shop that reports to nobody, appears in no report at any price. Use entries to test the seller's story, not as a pass/fail.

Service records

What to read: the gaps.

Regular entries then three years of silence usually means one of three things — the car sat, it was serviced somewhere that does not report, or it was off the road. Each has different consequences. Ask.

Lien records

What to read: whether any lien shows a release.

An open lien means someone other than the seller has a claim on the car. This is the section most likely to cost you the entire purchase price if you skip it. See how to avoid paying for someone else's loan.

Auction and sale records

What to read: sale prices and the condition grades, where present.

A car that moved through dealer auctions repeatedly at low prices was being passed around by people who inspect cars for a living. That is worth knowing.

Theft records

What to read: reported stolen, and whether recovered.

A recovered theft is not necessarily a problem. An unrecovered theft report on a car being offered to you is the end of the conversation.

The two-column methodBefore you open the report, write down what the seller told you: how many owners, what states, what accidents, what work was done. Then read the report against your own notes. You are not looking for a clean report. You are looking for a report that matches the story.

Pull the record and read it against what you were told.
