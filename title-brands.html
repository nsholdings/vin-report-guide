Title brands, title washing, and odometer fraud

Last checked September 6, 2026

A title brand is a permanent notation on a vehicle's title saying something significant happened to it. Brands are applied by state agencies, and the exact names and rules differ from state to state — which is the root of most of the trouble described on this page.

The brands you will run into

BrandWhat it means
SalvageAn insurer declared the vehicle a total loss — repair cost exceeded a threshold percentage of its value. Thresholds vary by state.
Rebuilt / ReconstructedA salvage vehicle that has been repaired and passed a state inspection to return to the road. It is roadworthy; it is not undamaged.
FloodDamaged by water. The concern is corrosion and electrical faults that surface months or years later.
Lemon / Manufacturer buybackRepurchased by the manufacturer under a state lemon law after repeated unresolved defects.
Junk / Non-repairableNot legally returnable to the road. It exists for parts.
Odometer discrepancyA recorded reading was inconsistent with an earlier one, or the odometer is known to be inaccurate.

Title washing

Because brands and thresholds differ between states, a vehicle branded in one state can sometimes be retitled in another and come out with a clean title. The damage did not go anywhere. The paperwork did.

This is precisely why a check against a single state's records is not enough, and why a report that traces title history across states is worth more than one that only tells you the current status.

Pattern worth noticingA car with several title transfers in a short window, across several states, and no clear reason. Legitimate cars do get moved and resold. But a fast multi-state path is the shape title washing leaves behind, and it deserves an explanation before you buy.

Odometer rollback

Digital odometers did not end this. They moved it — rolling back a digital cluster is a software job, and a replacement cluster can be fitted with any starting figure.

What defeats it is not inspecting the dashboard. It is having a series of readings recorded over time, from title transfers, inspections, and service visits. A single reading proves nothing. A sequence that goes 140,000 then 78,000 proves quite a lot.

Physical signs worth checking anyway

  • Wear on the pedals, steering wheel and driver's seat bolster that does not match the displayed mileage
  • Service stickers or inspection records showing a higher figure than the dash
  • Replacement tires on a car claiming very low mileage

These are supporting evidence, not proof. The record trail is the proof.

Title history across states and a sequence of recorded odometer readings are exactly what the free checks do not give you.
