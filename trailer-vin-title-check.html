Checking a used trailer before you buy it

Last checked November 1, 2026

A trailer is cheap enough that people skip the checks, and easy enough to steal that the checks matter. It also sits in an odd spot legally: the federal VIN rule covers it, the odometer rule does not, and whether it gets a title at all depends on its weight and the state.

Trailers do have real VINs

The federal VIN requirements apply to trailers, including trailer kits, alongside passenger cars, trucks and buses. A manufactured trailer therefore has a 17-character VIN assigned by its manufacturer, using only the permitted character set — A to Z without I, O and Q, plus 0 to 9 — with every position filled (49 CFR 565.13). That means you can read and check it the same way you would a car's: see decoding a VIN and the check digit.

What there is no record of is use. Federal law exempts any vehicle that is not self-propelled from odometer disclosure (49 CFR 580.17(a)(2)), and a trailer has no odometer to disclose. Age, condition and the frame are all you have.

Whether it has a title depends on weight and state

This varies, so check the rule where you will register it. Texas is a useful worked example of how the thresholds tend to be structured (TxDMV):

TrailerTexas requirement
Manufactured, over 4,000 lb gross weightMust be titled
Newly assembled, 4,000 lb or lessTitling optional
Newly assembled farm trailer, up to 34,000 lbTitling optional
Farm trailer over 34,000 lbMust be titled and registered

What the seller must hand you follows from that: a manufacturer's certificate of origin for a new trailer, a certificate of title for a used trailer over 4,000 lb, and a bill of sale plus the registration receipt for one of 4,000 lb or less — or the title, if one was ever issued.

"No title" is normal for a small trailer, and that is the problemBecause a light trailer may legitimately have none, a stolen one is easy to pass off with a handwritten bill of sale. Get the seller's ID, match it to whatever paperwork exists, and write the VIN into the document — see what a bill of sale should include.

Run the VIN before you pay. A theft or salvage record on a trailer is the record you most want to find first.

Homemade trailers, and the trap in them

An assembled trailer needs a VIN before it can be titled, and in Texas getting one requires an inspection by an auto theft law enforcement officer, arranged through a local law enforcement agency or a state service centre. The rule that matters to a buyer is the next one: a manufactured trailer with missing paperwork cannot be registered as an assembled trailer.

That closes a loophole, and it tells you what to watch for. A trailer presented as homemade that has factory welds, a stamped manufacturer plate, or a ground-off area where a plate used to be is probably a manufactured trailer whose papers are missing — and you will not be able to register it as either one.

The physical check

  • Find every VIN location. Usually a plate or sticker on the tongue or frame rail near the coupler. A missing plate, or fresh grinding near one, ends the conversation.
  • Frame and crossmembers. Rust at the welds and at the spring hangers is structural. Surface rust on a fender is not.
  • Coupler, safety chains and jack. Cheap to replace, and their condition tells you how the trailer was treated.
  • Axles, bearings and brakes. Jack a wheel and feel for play and roughness.
  • Tyres. Trailer tyres usually age out before they wear out. Check the date code moulded on the sidewall, not just the tread.
  • Lights and wiring. Hook it to a vehicle and test every function before you hand over money.

A seller who will not let you check the VIN against the paperwork is telling you something — see when the seller is not the person on the title. Boats follow a different scheme again: see checking a hull identification number.

Check the VIN for theft, salvage and title records before handing over cash for a trailer.
