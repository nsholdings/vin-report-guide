The dealer Buyers Guide sticker: what a used-car dealer must disclose

Last checked September 19, 2026

Every used car on a US dealer's lot is supposed to carry a Buyers Guide — the form hanging from the mirror or taped to a side window. It is the only disclosure federal law requires from the dealer, it becomes part of your contract, and most buyers never read it. Here is what it tells you, and what it does not.

Who has to post one

The requirement comes from the Federal Trade Commission's Used Car Rule, 16 CFR Part 455. The rule defines a dealer as anyone who sells or offers a used vehicle for sale "after selling or offering for sale five (5) or more used vehicles in the previous twelve months." A private individual selling their own car will normally fall outside that definition, so a private-party sale comes with no Buyers Guide at all.

  • Vehicle types. The rule covers cars, light-duty vans and trucks, demonstrators and program cars. The FTC's consumer guidance says dealers do not have to display a Guide on motorcycles and most recreational vehicles. Vehicles sold only for scrap or parts, with a salvage certificate issued, are also excluded.
  • Online purchases. The same FTC page says you are entitled to a Buyers Guide if you buy a used car online.
  • Two states differ. According to the FTC Dealer's Guide to the Used Car Rule, the rule applies in all states except Maine and Wisconsin, where dealers display their own state version.
  • Spanish-language sales. If the sale is conducted in Spanish, the Guide and the related contract disclosure must be in Spanish too.

The Guide must be posted before the car is offered for sale, in a place where both sides can be read. The FTC says a Guide kept in the glove compartment, trunk or under a seat is not conspicuous.

Reading the front: which box is checked

The front is about warranty coverage: who pays if something breaks after you drive away.

What the Guide showsWhat it means for you
As Is – No Dealer WarrantyThe dealer makes no promise to repair anything. If the transmission fails on the way home, the repair is yours.
Implied Warranties OnlyNo written warranty, but the implied warranties that come with a sale under state law still apply and may give you some rights over serious problems that were not apparent when you bought.
Dealer Warranty (Full or Limited)The dealer must list which systems are covered, for how long, and what percentage of parts and labor it pays. Any deductible has to be explained.
Non-dealer warranty boxesOptional boxes showing that the manufacturer's original warranty has not expired, or that a manufacturer's or other used-vehicle warranty applies.
Service contract boxA service contract is available for an extra charge. This is not a warranty.

Whether a dealer can sell "as is" at all depends on your state. The rule says state law overrides it where a state limits or prohibits "as is" sales, and the FTC tells buyers to ask their state attorney general what applies locally.

The service contract detail most buyers missThe FTC's consumer page says that if you buy a service contract from the dealer within 90 days of the sale, the dealer cannot disclaim implied warranties on the systems that contract covers — even on a car marked "as is".

The Guide overrides your contract

This is the part with teeth. Under the regulation, the information on the final version of the Guide is incorporated into the sales contract and "overrides any contrary provisions in the contract of sale." The contract itself must carry a sentence saying the window form is part of the contract.

In practice:

  • If the Guide on the car showed a 30-day warranty and the contract you are handed says "as is", the Guide wins. Compare the two before signing.
  • If you negotiated better coverage, the change has to be written on the Guide itself. The FTC's example is crossing out "50 percent" and writing "100 percent".
  • Spoken promises — "we'll fix that noise if it comes back" — are, in the Guide's own words, difficult to enforce. Get them written on the Guide.
There is no federal three-day returnThe FTC states that federal law does not require dealers to give you three days to cancel the deal and return the car. Once you sign, assume the sale is final unless the contract says otherwise.

What the Buyers Guide does not tell you

The Guide is a warranty disclosure, not a history disclosure. Nothing in the rule requires the dealer to tell you about prior accidents, flood damage, title brands, odometer problems or open liens. The rule does make it deceptive to misrepresent a car's mechanical condition — but silence is not misrepresentation.

The FTC knows this gap exists. When it revised the form in 2016, it added airbags and catalytic converters to the list of major defects on the back, and added a statement directing buyers to obtain a vehicle history report and check for open safety recalls.

So before you sign:

  1. Check open recalls by VIN at NHTSA. See what you can check for free.
  2. Pull a history report and read it against what the salesperson told you. See how to read a vehicle history report.
  3. Get an independent inspection. The FTC notes that a history report typically will not list mechanical problems, and that an inspection is a good idea even when the dealer says the car is "certified". If a dealer will not allow one, the FTC suggests considering another dealer.

The Buyers Guide covers the warranty. Title brands, odometer readings, reported damage and lien records come from the vehicle's history — which the dealer is not required to tell you about.

On the lot

Photograph both sides of the Guide before you negotiate. At signing, compare the contract with that photo, and make sure anything promised aloud has been written on the Guide.

Buying from a private seller instead? There is no Guide, no dealer warranty and fewer protections, so the record checks carry more weight. The pre-purchase checklist sets out the order.
