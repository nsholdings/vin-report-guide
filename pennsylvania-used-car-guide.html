Buying a used car in Pennsylvania: reconstructed, flood and recovered-theft titles

Last checked October 4, 2026

Pennsylvania's title system uses its own vocabulary. A rebuilt car is "reconstructed", a water-damaged one may be branded "flood", and a stolen-and-recovered car may or may not be branded depending on how much it cost to repair. Here is how to read a Pennsylvania title and what the state requires before a branded car goes back on the road.

The brands a Pennsylvania title can carry

PennDOT's application for branded titles, Form MV-426B, shows the brands that can appear on a Pennsylvania certificate of title: reconstructed, flood, recovered theft, and combinations such as reconstructed and flood, or reconstructed and recovered theft. A buyer should treat any of these words on the title as a reason to ask questions and dig into the history.

What "reconstructed" means

PennDOT's fact sheet on reconstructed vehicles defines a reconstructed vehicle as one, other than an antique or classic vehicle, for which a certificate of salvage was issued and which was then restored to operating condition to meet the vehicle equipment and inspection standards. In other words, the car was first a salvage vehicle, and the reconstructed title is the brand it carries after repair.

Whether such a car is a sensible buy depends on price and repair quality; see is a rebuilt title ever worth buying?

The enhanced vehicle safety inspection

Before Pennsylvania issues a branded title for a reconstructed, flood or recovered-theft vehicle, the car must pass an enhanced vehicle safety inspection. MV-426B states that only safety inspection stations appointed and under contract with PennDOT as Enhanced Vehicle Safety Inspection Stations may perform these inspections. PennDOT's fact sheet says the enhanced inspection is needed once, at initial titling; after that, routine inspections can be done at any station.

That means a reconstructed car you buy today may have had its enhanced inspection years ago. It tells you the car met the standard at that time, not that it does now. A pre-purchase inspection is still worth doing.

Pennsylvania brands follow the car into national title data. Check what's on record before you go to see it.

Why some recovered theft cars have no brand

MV-426B lists the possible situations for a recovered theft vehicle. One option on the form is a recovered theft vehicle where the cost of repairs is 50% or less of the replacement value, and the applicant acknowledges the title will not be branded as reconstructed or recovered theft. When repairs cost more than 50% of replacement value, the form leads to a recovered theft brand.

For a buyer, that means a stolen-and-recovered car can come with a plain Pennsylvania title. A theft record may still appear in other databases, which is one reason to run a VIN check even when the title looks clean. See recovered theft vehicles.

Flood: total loss or not

The same form separates a flood vehicle that was declared a total loss from one where the damage did not exceed the vehicle's replacement value. Either can lead to a flood brand on the Pennsylvania title. If you see it, find out how deep the water went and what was replaced — see how to spot a flood-damaged car.

Inspection after you buy

Pennsylvania's inspection regulations, quoted in PennDOT's Publication 45, say a vehicle that does not display a valid certificate of inspection at the time of sale must be inspected within 10 days of the sale. If the car you are buying has no current sticker, plan for that inspection and ask the seller why it lapsed.

PennDOT also tells buyers to check NMVTIS before you buy, noting that title brands describe a vehicle's prior condition, such as junk, salvage or flood.

Pennsylvania checklist

  1. Read the title for reconstructed, flood or recovered-theft brands.
  2. Remember the enhanced inspection happened once, at retitling.
  3. A recovered theft car may have no brand; run a VIN check anyway.
  4. No current inspection sticker? Budget for an inspection within 10 days.
  5. Get an independent inspection before paying.
