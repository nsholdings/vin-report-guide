How to avoid paying for someone else's car loan

Last checked September 13, 2026

This is the failure that costs the full purchase price. You buy the car, you pay the seller, and a lender who was never paid still holds a claim against the vehicle — which is now in your driveway.

What a lien is

When a car is bought with a loan, the lender records an interest in it. The lien is attached to the vehicle, not to the person. Until the loan is paid and the lender releases it, the lender has a claim regardless of who is currently holding the keys.

When the loan is paid off, the lender issues a release and the title is updated. The gap between "paid off" and "released and recorded" is where buyers get hurt.

How it goes wrong

  • The seller still owes money and intends to pay it off with your payment — and then does not
  • The loan was paid but the release was never recorded, so the title still shows the lien
  • The seller has a duplicate or reprinted title that does not show a lien recorded elsewhere
  • The car was bought in another state and the lien was recorded there

Checking before you pay

1. Read the physical title

Lienholder information is printed on the title. If a lienholder is listed, there must be a corresponding release. Look at the document itself, not a photo, not a photocopy.

2. Check the history report's lien section

Lien records appear in a full vehicle history report, including liens recorded in other states. This is the section most buyers skim past. See how to read a vehicle history report.

Lien records, title history across states and the odometer sequence come from the same report. This is the single check that can save the entire purchase price.

3. Ask the state motor vehicle agency

Procedures vary by state, but title status can generally be verified with the agency that issued it. If anything about the title looks unusual, this is worth the phone call.

Closing safely when a lien exists

A car with an outstanding loan can be bought safely — it happens constantly — but the payment has to go to the lender, not through the seller's hands.

  1. Ask the seller for the 10-day payoff quote from their lender, in writing, with the account number and the lender's payment instructions
  2. Pay the lender directly for the payoff amount, and pay the seller only the difference
  3. Where possible, close the transaction at the lender's branch — a bank or credit union that holds the title can release it on the spot
  4. Get a written bill of sale recording the payoff arrangement and both payments
  5. Do not consider the deal finished until the lien release is in your hands and the title is transferred
The sentence that should stop you"Just pay me and I'll take care of the loan." There is no version of this that protects you. If the seller will not let you pay their lender directly, the reason is never good.

The order that keeps you safe

  1. Pull the history report and read the lien section
  2. Read the physical title and compare the VIN to the car
  3. If a lien exists, get the payoff quote in writing
  4. Pay the lender directly; pay the seller the difference
  5. Take delivery only with the release and title in hand
