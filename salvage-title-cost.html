What a salvage title actually costs you

Last checked September 13, 2026

A salvage-title car is cheap for a reason, and the reason is not only the damage. The brand follows the car permanently, and it changes what you can do with it.

What the brand means

An insurer declared the vehicle a total loss — the cost to repair exceeded a threshold percentage of its value. That threshold is set by state law and differs between states, which is why an identical car can be branded in one state and not in another. The brand does not describe how bad the damage was. It describes an economic decision made by an insurer about a specific car at a specific moment.

A salvage vehicle that has been repaired and passed a state inspection typically gets a different brand — rebuilt, reconstructed, or a state-specific equivalent — and can return to the road. It never goes back to clean.

The four costs that follow the brand

1. Insurance

Liability coverage is generally available. Comprehensive and collision are where it gets difficult: some insurers decline branded vehicles outright, and others will cover them at a reduced valuation, because the car's value is already reduced. The practical consequence is that if you total it again, the payout is small.

Do this before buying, not after: call your own insurer with the VIN and ask what they will write on that specific car. Get the answer before you hand over money, because you cannot legally drive it home without coverage.

2. Financing

Many lenders will not write a loan against a branded title, because the collateral is hard to value and hard to resell. In practice, salvage and rebuilt purchases are often cash transactions. Budget accordingly.

3. Resale

You inherit the same problem you are benefiting from now. The pool of buyers for a branded car is smaller and more price-sensitive, and every one of them will discount for the brand the way you are discounting today. The discount does not wear off with time.

4. Inspection and unknowns

You are buying repair work done by someone whose incentives you do not know, to a standard you cannot see. Frame repairs, airbag replacement and suspension geometry are the expensive things to get wrong and the hard things to verify visually.

The airbag questionIf the car was in a collision severe enough to total it, ask specifically whether the airbags deployed and what was done about them. Confirm the system shows no fault on startup, and that a mechanic — not the seller — has verified the modules. This is not a place to accept a verbal answer.

When the math works anyway

  • You are paying cash and do not need comprehensive coverage
  • You intend to keep the car until it dies, so resale is irrelevant
  • The damage was documented, cosmetic or localized, and you have seen the repair record
  • An independent mechanic has inspected it specifically for structural repair
  • The discount is large enough to absorb the resale hit you will take later

If several of those are not true, the discount is compensating you for risk you have not priced.

Before you get as far as the insurance call, confirm what the title history actually says — including brands applied in other states.
