Checking a used electric car: what the history shows and what it cannot

Last checked November 1, 2026

An electric car needs every check a petrol car needs, plus one that no history report can perform. The records answer the question of what has happened to the car. They do not answer the question that decides what a used EV is worth, which is how much of the battery is left.

What the history report covers, unchanged

Title brands, salvage and flood notations, the odometer sequence, prior commercial use, liens and open recalls all work exactly as they do on any other vehicle. An EV is titled and branded by the same state agencies under the same rules. If anything, water damage deserves more weight rather than less, given how much of the car is high-voltage electrical — see flood-damaged cars.

Recalls matter especially here. Battery-pack recalls have been among the larger EV campaigns, and some have involved charging restrictions until the remedy was performed. The federal lookup is free and takes seconds: see open recalls on used cars.

What no report will tell you

  • Battery state of health. Not a reported field. It lives in the car.
  • Charging history. How often it was fast-charged, or left at a high state of charge in heat, is logged by the car, not by any database.
  • Whether the pack or modules were replaced — unless it happened through a recall or a warranty claim that a report happens to carry.

Start with the records that do exist — title brands, odometer entries, liens and prior use — then test the battery separately.

What the evidence says about EV batteries generally

This is worth knowing before you assume the worst. The EPA states that EV drivetrain batteries are designed to last the lifetime of the vehicle and have very low failure rates: in the data it cites, 97.5% of EVs still had their original battery outside of major recalls, and for model year 2016 onward the failure rate was under 0.5% (EPA). The same page notes that batteries do tend to lose some of their initial range over time.

So the realistic risk on a used EV is usually not a dead pack. It is gradual range loss, which is normal, varies by car and by how it was used, and is not a defect. What you are buying is the remaining range — so measure it rather than guessing.

How to check the battery on the car

  1. Ask for a battery health report from a franchised dealer for that brand. Many can pull a state-of-health figure from the car's own systems. This is the single most useful document you can ask for.
  2. Charge it to full and read the indicated range, then compare against the car's original rating for that model year and trim. Indicated range is an estimate that reacts to recent driving, so treat it as an indication, not a measurement.
  3. Look at the car's own battery screens. Many EVs display state of health, usable capacity or a degradation figure directly.
  4. Drive it on the motorway, not just around the block. Range loss shows up fastest at speed.
  5. Check the charging hardware. Port, latch, cable and the onboard charger. Test both AC charging and a DC fast charger if you can.
Degradation is not the same as failureA pack that has lost range is behaving as designed. A pack with a fault, a warning light, or a charging restriction is a different conversation, and a far more expensive one.

Warranty is the other half of the price

Battery and high-voltage component coverage is set by each manufacturer, differs by brand and model year, and can differ by state. Some coverage does not pass to a second owner. There is no single rule to rely on, so read that model year's warranty booklet and have a franchised dealer confirm the remaining coverage against the VIN — the method is in does the factory warranty transfer.

And still put it on a lift. Suspension, brakes, tyres, underbody damage and the state of the pack's casing are all ordinary mechanical questions: see the pre-purchase inspection.

Check the title, brand and recall history against the VIN before you spend a day testing the battery.
