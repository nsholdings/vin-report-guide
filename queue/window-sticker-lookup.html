The original window sticker: what it proves about a used car

Last checked November 22, 2026

When a seller tells you a used car is the higher trim, or that a feature came from the factory rather than being added later, there is a document that settles it: the original window sticker. Federal law dictates what it has to contain, and it is tied to that specific vehicle.

What the law requires on it

The label is usually called a Monroney sticker, after the statute at 15 U.S.C. 1231–1233. Every manufacturer of new automobiles distributed in commerce must "securely affix to the windshield, or side window" of each new car a label whose entries are "true and correct," before delivery to a dealer (15 U.S.C. 1232).

The required contents, as the statute lists them:

  • the make, model, and serial or identification number;
  • the final assembly point;
  • the name and business location of the dealer the car is to be delivered to, and the city or town of that delivery;
  • the method of transportation if the car was driven or towed from the assembly point;
  • the manufacturer's suggested retail price; the suggested retail delivered price of each accessory or item of optional equipment physically attached at delivery and not already included; the transportation charge to the dealer; and the total of those amounts.

Why that matters to a used buyer

Two of those items do the work. The label is tied to the vehicle's serial or identification number, and it itemizes each piece of optional equipment physically attached at delivery, with its price. Together that makes the original sticker a VIN-specific, manufacturer-certified list of the factory options actually on that car when it left the assembly point — and a willfully false endorsement of the label is a federal offence carrying a fine of up to $1,000 per automobile (15 U.S.C. 1233(b)).

So if the sticker does not list a sunroof, a tow package or a particular audio system, that equipment was not on the car at delivery. It may have been added properly afterwards — or it may be the seller's description running ahead of the facts.

A factory options list only helps if the car's recorded history matches it. Check both.

Safety ratings are on the label too

Where NHTSA has assigned and published New Car Assessment Program ratings, the statute requires the label to carry a graphic showing the rating with the maximum possible rating clearly differentiated, the rating categories, an explanation of the crash-test data with a pointer to further resources including a website, and prominent placement covering at least 8 percent of the label's total area, or an area at least 4.5 inches long by 3.5 inches high (15 U.S.C. 1232(g)). If the vehicle was not tested, or not rated in a category, the label must say so (1232(h)).

NHTSA's own consumer-information regulations sit at 49 CFR Part 575, which includes vehicle labeling of safety rating information and of fuel economy and emissions information.

When the sticker legally comes off

Willfully removing, altering or making the label illegible "prior to the time that [the] automobile is delivered to the actual custody and possession of the ultimate purchaser" carries a fine of up to $1,000, up to a year of imprisonment, or both, with each automobile a separate offence (15 U.S.C. 1233(c)). The practical effect: the sticker stays on the car until the first retail buyer takes possession, and lawfully comes off then. Which is why used cars almost never have one.

No legal right to a reprintThe statute's duties run only to a "new automobile" — one whose title has never been transferred by a manufacturer, distributor or dealer to an ultimate purchaser (15 U.S.C. 1231). Nothing in these laws requires a manufacturer to reprint, archive or supply a window sticker for a used car, or to make one retrievable from a VIN. Some manufacturers offer a lookup as a courtesy; that is a business choice, not an obligation, and third-party reprint services are not official.

What else is on a modern sticker

The EPA Fuel Economy and Environment label, which appears on the same sticker, carries city, highway and combined MPG with the combined figure most prominent, a comparison to the best and worst in the vehicle's class, a five-year fuel cost comparison against the average new vehicle at 15,000 miles a year, estimated annual fuel cost, a 1-to-10 fuel economy and greenhouse gas rating, combined tailpipe CO2 in grams per mile, a 1-to-10 smog rating, and a QR code (EPA).

Using it in a purchase

  1. Ask the seller whether they have the original sticker. Some owners keep it with the manuals.
  2. Match the identification number on the sticker to the VIN on the car. A sticker for a different car proves nothing.
  3. Compare the itemized options to what you can see. Extra equipment that is not on the list was added later.
  4. Check the trim claim against the model line and options, not against the badge on the trunk.

Note that the statute requires "make, model" — not the trim name as such. Trim follows from the model plus the itemized options, and the label does not have to state the build date, engine or colour.

Confirm the trim and the history before you agree a price based on either.
