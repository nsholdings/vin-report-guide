Buying a used car in New York: the rebuilt salvage brand, the salvage exam and the used car lemon law

Last checked October 4, 2026

New York does three things that matter to a used-car buyer: it brands rebuilt salvage titles only on newer vehicles, it examines rebuilt salvage cars for stolen parts before retitling them, and it gives dealer buyers a statutory used car warranty. Each comes with a limit you need to know.

The "REBUILT SALVAGE: NY" brand — and the age limit

The New York DMV explains that a title is branded when a vehicle is 8 model years old or newer and was destroyed or damaged to 75% or more of its retail value. For those vehicles, the title shows "REBUILT SALVAGE: NY". The same page states that titles for vehicles older than 8 model years are not branded.

The consequence is easy to miss. An older car can have been badly damaged and rebuilt in New York with nothing on its title to show it. On an older car, a clean New York title tells you less than you might assume — check the VIN against national title and insurance data and get an inspection. See title brands and title washing.

What the DMV salvage examination is — and isn't

Salvage vehicles that have been rebuilt for road use must pass a DMV salvage vehicle examination before the DMV issues a new title or registration. It applies to vehicles with a New York Salvage Certificate (MV-907A) and to rebuilt salvage vehicles from most other states.

The DMV is explicit about its purpose. It is not a safety inspection, an emissions inspection or an insurance examination. New York law requires the DMV to determine whether a rebuilt salvage vehicle is stolen or contains stolen parts, and investigators from the DMV's auto theft and salvage unit carry out the exam.

Passing the exam says nothing about the repairA car can pass the salvage exam with poor structural or airbag repairs, because those are not what the exam looks at. Get an independent inspection — see the pre-purchase inspection.

The seller's damage and odometer statement

New York's Odometer and Damage Disclosure Statement (MV-103) asks the seller to certify whether the cost to rebuild or reconstruct the vehicle is more than 75% of its retail value. It also has the seller state the mileage or mark it as exceeding mechanical limits or "not the actual mileage". The form warns that falsifying the information may lead to fines or imprisonment.

The DMV's consumer brochure adds that a person or dealer who sells a rebuilt salvage vehicle must provide the buyer a disclosure statement regardless of the vehicle's age. Read what you are given before you sign; if a box about prior damage is checked, ask what happened. For mileage, see odometer rollback.

New York does not brand older rebuilt cars. A VIN report can show brands, total-loss records and auction history from other sources.

The used car lemon law: who it covers

The New York Attorney General's used car lemon law fact sheet sets out the main conditions. The car must have been bought or leased from a New York dealer, have had fewer than 100,000 miles when you bought it, cost at least $1,500, and be used primarily for personal purposes. The Attorney General's guide defines a dealer as someone who sold or leased three or more used vehicles in the previous twelve months. A private sale between individuals is not covered.

The dealer's written warranty runs for a period that depends on the mileage at purchase, whichever limit comes first:

Mileage at purchaseMinimum warranty
18,001–36,00090 days or 4,000 miles
36,001–79,99960 days or 3,000 miles
80,000–100,00030 days or 1,000 miles

Under the Attorney General's guide, the remedy becomes available when the same defect has been subject to repair three or more times by the dealer within the warranty period, among other conditions. Keep every repair order. If something goes wrong after you buy, see you bought the car and found a problem.

New York checklist

  1. On a car older than 8 model years, don't treat a clean title as proof of no major damage.
  2. Read the MV-103 damage and odometer statement before signing.
  3. Remember the salvage exam is an anti-theft check, not a safety check.
  4. Buying from a dealer? Confirm the lemon-law warranty terms in writing.
  5. Run a VIN check and get an independent inspection.
