The pre-purchase inspection: what it covers and how to arrange one

Last checked September 20, 2026

A pre-purchase inspection, or PPI, is a mechanic you choose examining the car before you buy it. It is the most expensive check in the process, which is why it should come last — after the paperwork has already ruled out the cars that were never going to work. See the checklist for the order.

What a typical inspection covers

  • Engine: leaks, noises, fluid condition, diagnostic trouble codes
  • Transmission and drivetrain
  • Brakes and suspension, with the car on a lift
  • Steering components
  • Tires: wear pattern and age
  • Body: evidence of accident repair, rust, panel alignment
  • Electrical systems and warning lights
  • A road test

What is included varies by shop. Ask for a written list before you book, and ask for the results in writing.

Tell the mechanic what to look for

An inspection is better when it is targeted. Before the appointment, share what you already know:

  • A reported accident in the history → ask them to look specifically for structural and frame repair. See frame damage.
  • A car from a flood-prone area, or a flood report → ask them to check for water intrusion. See flood damage.
  • A branded title → ask them to assess the quality of the repair. See rebuilt titles.
  • Mileage that looks inconsistent → ask about wear relative to the odometer. See odometer rollback.

Pull the history first, then hand it to your mechanic. A targeted inspection finds more.

Choose the mechanic yourself

Use a shop with no connection to the seller. A seller who insists on "their" mechanic, or who offers a recent inspection report from a shop you did not choose, is offering something much less useful than an independent inspection.

If the seller won't allow it

Some sellers refuse. A few have understandable reasons — they don't want strangers driving the car around town. The usual compromises:

  • A mobile inspector who comes to the car
  • The seller drives the car to the shop and waits
  • A shop close to the seller

A seller who refuses every version of this is usually telling you something about the car.

For a car you can't visit

Arrange a mobile or local inspection in the seller's area and have the report sent directly to you. See before you travel to buy a car.

Use the report to negotiateFindings that don't kill the deal — worn brakes, aging tires — are documented costs. A written estimate from the inspecting shop is a reasonable basis for a price adjustment.
