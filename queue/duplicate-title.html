Buying a car with a duplicate title

Last checked September 20, 2026

Titles are lost, damaged and misplaced constantly, and state agencies issue replacements routinely. Most duplicate titles are exactly what they appear to be. A few are used to sell a car that still has a loan against it. The difference is worth a few minutes.

How duplicates are marked

States typically mark replacement titles as duplicates, reprints or replacements, and the exact wording and format vary by state. If you are unsure whether a title is an original or a replacement, your state's motor vehicle agency can tell you what to look for.

The risk: a lien that isn't on the paper

Here is how it goes wrong. A car is financed, and the lender's interest is recorded. The owner then obtains a replacement title — and depending on how and where that happened, the document in the seller's hand may not show the lien that still exists. You pay, the loan goes unpaid, and the lender still has a claim on the car. See how to avoid paying for someone else's loan.

What to check

  1. The issue date. A duplicate issued very recently, just before the sale, is worth asking about.
  2. The lien section on the title itself.
  3. The lien records in a history report — which can show liens recorded in other states, not only on the paper in front of you.
  4. The name on the title against the seller's ID.

Lien records in the history report are independent of whatever the paper title shows.

Ask the direct question

"Why is this a duplicate?" A plausible answer — lost in a move, damaged — is normal. Hesitation, or a story that changes, is not.
