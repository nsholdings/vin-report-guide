Title washing: how a branded car gets a clean title in another state

Last checked November 22, 2026

A used car's title can carry a permanent mark — salvage, junk, flood, rebuilt — that tells you something serious once happened to it. Those marks are assigned by states, and states do not all use the same ones. That gap is what makes "title washing" possible: a vehicle branded in one state is retitled in another, and the new paper comes out clean.

What title washing actually is

The U.S. Department of Justice, which oversees the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System (NMVTIS), describes the problem directly. On its consumer page it explains that a brand such as junk, salvage or flood can be removed when a vehicle is retitled in a state that does not check the earlier record, and calls the practice brand washing (vehiclehistory.bja.ojp.gov).

The same page cites an Experian Automotive figure from the first half of 2008: more than 185,000 titles that had been branded in one state were retitled elsewhere as clean. That number is from 2008 and describes that period only — it is not a current measurement, and no federal source we could find publishes a newer count.

Why it works at all

States set their own branding standards. DOJ says so plainly on the same page, which is why a vehicle one state would brand as salvage may not carry that brand somewhere else. There is no single federal definition that every state applies to the car in front of you. See what the common title brands mean for how far apart those labels can sit.

A brand removed by one state can still sit in the national record. Check the title history across states.

What federal law requires states to do

The Anti-Car Theft Act created NMVTIS in 1992, and a 1996 amendment moved the program from the Department of Transportation to DOJ (bja.ojp.gov). The anti-washing duty sits in the statute: each state is to perform an "instant title verification check" before issuing a certificate of title to someone who bought the vehicle from another state, sending the VIN, the state that issued the most recent title, and the current titleholder's name (49 U.S.C. 30503(b)).

The regulation puts a date on it. By January 1, 2010, a state was to run the NMVTIS check before issuing a title on an out-of-state purchase or transfer, and use the result to check whether the title document is valid and current, whether the vehicle "is or has been reported as junk or salvage," and to compare and verify the odometer information presented (28 CFR 25.54(c)).

The part that protects you

Brands cannot be erased inside NMVTIS itself. The regulation says the system operator "may not allow any entity to delete a prior report of junk or salvage status" (28 CFR 25.57(b)). That is the structural reason a washed state title can still be contradicted by the national record: the clean piece of paper is new, but the earlier report is still there.

States must also send their titling data to the operator once every 24 hours, including the description on the certificate of title and "any and all brands" (28 CFR 25.54(a)).

What the law does not doNo federal source we reviewed says a state must carry an incoming brand forward. The regulation requires the check and says the state uses the result — it does not say what the state must do with a hit. The check is a safeguard, not a guarantee.

How a buyer uses this

  • Do not treat the title in the seller's hand as the whole history. It reflects one state's decision at one moment.
  • Look at brand history across states, not just the current title. A consumer NMVTIS report covers the current state of title and last title date, brand history, the odometer reading, total loss history and salvage history (DOJ).
  • Be suspicious of a recent out-of-state retitle on a car whose age and condition do not match a clean record — especially where the previous state is one with a different branding standard.
  • Still inspect the car. DOJ's own disclaimer says a vehicle history report "is NOT a substitute for an independent vehicle inspection."

One honest limit

Nothing in the federal material we reviewed makes title washing a named federal crime in itself, and none of it lists which states fall short on the verification check. The practical defence is the same as it has always been: check the national record rather than the local paperwork, and have the car inspected.
