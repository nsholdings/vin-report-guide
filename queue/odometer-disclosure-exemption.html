Which used cars still require a written odometer disclosure

Last checked November 22, 2026

"That car's old enough that mileage doesn't have to be disclosed" is one of those statements that is sometimes right and often wrong. The federal rule is more specific than the version that gets repeated, and the difference matters precisely on the cars most private buyers are looking at.

The rule, as written

The exemptions live at 49 CFR 580.17 — not 580.6, which covers additional requirements for electronic disclosure. There are two age tracks, split at model year 2010/2011:

  • §580.17(a)(3)(i) exempts "A vehicle manufactured in or before the 2010 model year" that is "transferred at least 10 years after January 1 of the calendar year corresponding to its designated model year."
  • §580.17(a)(4)(i) exempts "A vehicle manufactured in or after the 2011 model year" that is "transferred at least 20 years after January 1 of the calendar year corresponding to its designated model year."

The regulation supplies its own worked examples: "For vehicle transfers occurring during calendar year 2020, model year 2010 or older vehicles are exempt," and "For vehicle transfers occurring during calendar year 2031, model year 2011 or older vehicles are exempt" (49 CFR 580.17; same text in the GPO annual edition, PDF).

Why "20 years old" is the wrong shorthandThe 20-year span applies only to model year 2011 and later. For model year 2010 and earlier the span is 10 years. So the 2005–2010 model years became exempt under the 10-year track, long before any 20-year test would have reached them. Anyone stating a single flat number is interpreting, not quoting — the regulation never states one.

What that works out to right now

Applying the regulation's own formula to calendar year 2026 — our arithmetic, not a quotation — every model year 2010 and older vehicle is exempt, because the 10-year span had run out by 2020 at the latest. Every model year 2011 and later vehicle still requires a written disclosure, because the earliest the 20-year track expires is model year 2011 in 2031.

The consequence is unusual and worth understanding: the pool of exempt vehicles is frozen at model year 2010 and does not grow again until January 1, 2031. For the next several years, the line between "disclosure required" and "exempt" sits in exactly the same place.

An exempt car is the one where the paperwork protects you least. Check the recorded mileage history.

Where the 20-year track came from

NHTSA created it in the final rule "Odometer Disclosure Requirements," Docket NHTSA-2019-0127, RIN 2127-AL39 (Federal Register, Nov 26, 2019). That rule amended only §580.17; it did not change §580.5 or §580.6. The document's own dates are not entirely consistent — it gives an effective date of December 31, 2019 for the electronic-disclosure amendments while its summary puts the 20-year exemption change at January 1, 2021.

The other exemptions

Age is not the only one. Section 580.17(a) also exempts vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating of more than 16,000 pounds, vehicles that are not self-propelled, and vehicles sold directly by the manufacturer to a U.S. federal agency under contract specifications. Subsection (b) exempts a transferor of a new vehicle prior to its first transfer for purposes other than resale.

Note which direction that federal-agency exemption runs: it covers the manufacturer selling to an agency. It does not exempt the government's later resale of that vehicle to the public — see buying an ex-federal fleet vehicle.

What a disclosure has to contain

Where it is required, §580.5(c) says the transferor must disclose the mileage on the title itself, or on a reassignment document where the transferor is not the titled owner. The disclosure must be signed and must include the odometer reading without tenths, the date of transfer, both parties' names and addresses, and the vehicle's identifying information. Section 580.5(h) bars one person from signing as both transferor and transferee in the same transaction except where the regulation allows it.

What this means when you are buying

  1. Model year 2011 or later: expect a signed mileage disclosure on the title. If the seller does not want to write one, that is a question worth asking out loud.
  2. Model year 2010 or older: no federal disclosure is required, so the paperwork gives you nothing on mileage. The protection has to come from the record and the car.
  3. Read the reading without tenths and compare it to every recorded mileage you can find across the car's life — inspection records, service records, prior title entries.
  4. State law may ask for more. Part 580 says nothing about state requirements that go beyond the federal rule, and we have not surveyed them.

Scale of the problem

NHTSA states that it "estimates that more than 450,000 vehicles are sold each year with false odometer readings," and its page prints a figure of "452K — Approximate number of odometer fraud cases per year in the United States" along with "3.47% — Chance of odometer fraud occurring during the first 11 years of a vehicle's life" (NHTSA). The page gives no date or methodology for those figures. It also summarises the exemption loosely, saying a vehicle is exempt "if it's 20 years old or older" while separately listing "a model year 2010 vehicle or older" — which is a reasonable illustration of why the regulation itself is the thing to read.
