The federal emissions warranty on a used car

Last checked November 22, 2026

Most buyers know the manufacturer's bumper-to-bumper warranty is probably long gone on a used car. Fewer know there is a separate federal emissions warranty, that it runs longer on specific parts, and that EPA says buying the car used does not take you out of it.

Two warranties, not one

The Clean Air Act requires manufacturers to provide two distinct warranties. The Performance Warranty "covers repairs which are required during the first 2 years or 24,000 miles of vehicle use (whichever first occurs) because the vehicle failed an emission test." The Design and Defect Warranty "covers repair of emission related parts which become defective during the warranty period" (EPA-420-F-09-048, PDF).

The coverage periods

The statute sets them for light-duty vehicles and trucks of model year 1995 and later. The shorter period "shall be the first 2 years or 24,000 miles of use (whichever first occurs)." The longer period, for a "specified major emission control component," "shall be 8 years or 80,000 miles of use (whichever first occurs)" (42 U.S.C. 7541(i)).

The statute's long-coverage list is short and closed: a "specified major emission control component" "means only a catalytic converter, an electronic emissions control unit," and "an onboard emissions diagnostic device." The Administrator may add devices only where they were not in general use before model year 1990 and their retail cost excluding installation exceeds $200 in 1989 dollars, adjusted for inflation.

The regulation's list is broader than the statute'sUnder the current regulation, eight-year/80,000-mile coverage attaches to catalytic converters and SCR catalysts and related components, particulate filters and traps, EGR components for compression-ignition engines, the emission control module, and certain electric-vehicle and plug-in hybrid batteries (40 CFR 85.2103(d)(1)). Attribute each list to its own source rather than merging them.

The regulation also sets the medium-duty figures: a five-year or 50,000-mile performance warranty and the same for the defect warranty (40 CFR 85.2103(c)(2), (d)(2)). And it states the obvious but useful rule that "The warranty period expires based on the specified age or mileage, whichever comes first."

Coverage runs on the vehicle's age and mileage — so the recorded mileage history matters.

Why this matters to a used buyer

EPA's consumer guidance is direct: "It does not matter if you bought your vehicle new or used from a dealer or anyone else," and "As long as the vehicle has not exceeded the warranty time or mileage limitations, these warranties apply." EPA's own FAQ adds that the warranty period starts at the sale to the original owner.

The statutory hook is in the Clean Air Act's wording, which requires a remedy where a nonconformity results in "the ultimate purchaser (or any subsequent purchaser)" having to bear a penalty or other sanction under state or federal law. Note that the statute does not contain an explicit "transfer" clause — the coverage follows the vehicle because of how the duty is written and how EPA reads it, not because a transfer provision says so.

So on a car first sold within the last eight years and under 80,000 miles, a failed catalytic converter or a dead engine control module is worth asking about before you pay for the repair yourself. This is general information, not a legal opinion on your specific vehicle.

The conditions

The statute attaches three requirements: the vehicle is "maintained and operated in accordance with instructions" from the manufacturer; it fails to conform during the warranty period to the applicable emission regulations; and that nonconformity results in the purchaser having to bear a penalty or other sanction. EPA's consumer guidance restates the practical version — the vehicle failed an approved emissions test, your state or local government requires you to repair it, the vehicle is within the time and mileage limits, the failure did not come from misuse, and the vehicle was properly maintained.

On proof: the regulation says a manufacturer may request maintenance records only where it has "an objective reason for believing" the work was not performed, and acceptable proof includes a maintenance log, professional service records, or an owner statement showing "the owner purchased and used proper parts" (40 CFR 85.2104). A claim "may be raised immediately upon the failure of an EPA-approved emission test" and may be brought to any repair facility the manufacturer authorises to service that model.

Denial is barred for "Any cause attributable to the vehicle manufacturer" and for "The use of any fuel which is commonly available in the geographical area" unless the instructions say otherwise.

California is longer

For 1990-and-newer passenger cars, light-duty trucks and medium-duty vehicles, California sets a defects and performance warranty of "3 years/50k miles" covering all emissions-related parts, and a high-cost emissions-related parts warranty of "7 years/ 70k miles" for specific parts listed in the owner's manual. PZEV-certified vehicles from 2001–2017 and TZEV-certified vehicles from 2018–2025 carry 15 years/150k miles for most emissions parts and 10 years/150k miles for the battery or other energy storage device (CARB). That fact sheet speaks only to California-certified vehicles; we have not checked the other states that follow California standards.

What to do with this

  1. Work out the vehicle's age from first sale and its mileage, not from the model year alone.
  2. Ask for maintenance records — they are the thing a manufacturer may ask for.
  3. If the car has a check-engine light or a failed test, establish which part failed before you agree a price. See emissions inspection history.
  4. Do not confuse this with the factory warranty. Whether the factory warranty transfers is a separate question with separate rules.
  5. On an electric or plug-in hybrid, the battery provisions above are the reason to establish the in-service date — see checking a used EV's history.

Check the car's reported mileage and title history before counting on any coverage.
