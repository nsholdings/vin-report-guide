You bought the car and found a problem. What now?

Last checked September 20, 2026

It happens after the sale: a warning light, a mechanic who finds old frame repair, a history report you ran too late. What you can do depends on who sold it, what was written down, and what state you are in. What follows is the order that preserves your options. It is not legal advice — for a specific dispute, talk to your state's consumer protection office or an attorney.

Step 1 — Stop and document

Before repairs, before arguments: record the problem.

  • Photograph and date it
  • Get a written diagnosis from an independent mechanic, stating what is wrong and, where they can tell, whether it predates the sale
  • Keep every document from the purchase: ad, messages, bill of sale, title, contract, and — from a dealer — the Buyers Guide

Screenshots of the original listing are worth taking now, before it disappears.

Step 2 — Pull the record if you have not

If the issue is an undisclosed accident, flood history, title brand or odometer discrepancy, a history report documents what was on record before you bought. That matters, because the question is often whether the problem was knowable — or known — at the time of sale.

Establish what was on record before the sale. The report documents the vehicle's title, odometer, accident and theft history as recorded.

Step 3 — Work out which situation you are in

Bought from a dealer

Read the Buyers Guide. Its terms are incorporated into the sales contract, and it states whether the car was sold as-is or with a warranty. If the Guide showed a warranty, the dealer's obligations follow from it. Whether a dealer may sell as-is at all depends on your state. See the dealer Buyers Guide.

Bought privately

Private sales usually carry no warranty. Your position turns much more on what the seller told you — which is why written messages matter. A seller who stated something false about the car is in a different position from one who simply stayed silent.

It's a recall

Check NHTSA Recalls. NHTSA states that dealers must honor recalls and remedy defects at no charge regardless of where the vehicle was originally purchased — so an open recall on a car you bought privately is still repaired free. See unrepaired recalls.

It's a title problem

An undisclosed brand, an unreleased lien or a title the seller could not legally transfer are different in kind from a mechanical fault. Contact your state's motor vehicle agency first.

Step 4 — Put it in writing to the seller

State the problem, what you found, the evidence, and what you are asking for. Keep it factual. A dated written record of the request matters if it escalates.

Step 5 — Know where to escalate

  • Your state attorney general's consumer protection office
  • The state agency that licenses dealers, for a dealer sale
  • Small claims court, depending on the amount and your state's limits
