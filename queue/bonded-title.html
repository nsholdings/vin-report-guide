The seller has no title: what a bonded title means for a buyer

Last checked November 22, 2026

Sooner or later a private seller will tell you the title is lost, or that they bought the car years ago and never got one. One legitimate route out of that is a bonded title: the applicant buys a surety bond, the state issues a title backed by it, and anyone with a prior claim can make a claim against the bond. The details vary so much by state that the only safe approach is to read the rules of the state involved.

Texas

The Texas DMV requires a bond "equal to 1 ½ times the value of the vehicle," with the value taken from the department's Standard Presumptive Value, or the NADA reference guide if there is no SPV, or a licensed dealer or insurance adjuster appraisal on Form VTR-125 submitted within 30 days of the appraisal (TxDMV).

The applicant must be a Texas resident or military stationed in Texas, must have possession, and the vehicle "cannot be junked, nonrepairable, or otherwise ineligible for a title." It must be complete with frame, body and motor, though it need not run. There is a one-year window from the notice date to buy the bond, then 30 days to apply for title, and a $15 administrative fee. For vehicles 25 years or older, appraisals under $4,000 are set at $4,000.

Georgia

Georgia runs a materially different calculation. The bond must equal "two times the average retail value of the vehicle," applied "Regardless of condition," and cannot be less than $5,000 (Georgia DOR). The owner must be a legal resident of Georgia, the vehicle "Cannot be a 1985 or older year model vehicle," and it "Cannot be an abandoned vehicle." The title application must be made within six months of the bond's issue date, with an $18 title fee.

That 1985 cut-off is worth pausing on, because Texas goes the other way and has a special rule for vehicles 25 years and older. Two states, opposite treatment of the same old car.

A missing title is a reason to look harder at the vehicle's recorded history, not less.

Washington — and the part that affects the buyer most

Washington also requires a bond "equal to 1-1/2 times the value of the vehicle as determined by the Department of Licensing" (Vehicle Ownership Bond, TD-420-054). The bond protects "any prior owner and secured party and any subsequent purchaser," indemnifying them against "any expense, loss or damage, including reasonable attorney's fees."

Washington states a duration where other states do not. The original bond goes to DOL within six months of issue, and it is returned after three years if the vehicle is no longer registered in Washington and the certificate of ownership is surrendered — unless an action to recover on the bond has been reported. During that period "an unrestricted title will not be issued for a period of three years" (TD-420-008).

And the clause a buyer needs to see: under the state's adopted rule, if ownership transfers during that period, the new owner must apply for title and "complete the time remaining in the current ownership in doubt period" (WAC 308-56A-210(7)). Buy a Washington car still inside its ownership-in-doubt window and you inherit the remainder of it.

Do not assume "three years" everywhereOnly Washington's own forms state that duration. The Texas and Georgia pages state application deadlines, not how long the bond stays in force. Virginia's titling page describes no bonded-title option at all — its only no-title workaround is an application in lieu of title limited to certain out-of-state trailers (Virginia DMV). Rules vary; check the state you are actually buying in.

What the official pages do not tell a buyer

Every one of these pages is written for the applicant — the person trying to get a title — not for the person buying the car afterwards. None of them explains what a purchaser should do when offered a bonded title, states what a surety actually charges for the bond, or says whether another state will honour an incoming bonded title. Treat anyone who speaks confidently about those points as giving you an opinion.

A buyer's checklist

  1. Ask whether a title exists today — not whether one is "being sorted out." A bonded title that has not been issued yet is not a title.
  2. Find out which state issued it and read that state's own page, because the bond multiplier, minimum, age limits and timing all differ.
  3. Ask whether a restricted period is still running. In Washington it transfers with the car.
  4. Check the vehicle record independently. A missing title can be innocent, or it can be the sign of a car whose paperwork someone wants to leave behind. Title jumping covers the version where the seller never titled it at all.
  5. Check for a lien. Texas will not accept an application where the applicant cannot get a lien release from a recorded lienholder whose lien is ten years old or less. See checking for a lien.
  6. If the story is simply a lost title, a duplicate title is usually the normal route, not a bond.

Check what the national record says about the car before you accept unusual paperwork.
