Buying a used car in New Jersey: the used car lemon law, flood titles and salvage

Last checked October 4, 2026

New Jersey gives used-car buyers some specific protections in law: dealers must provide a written warranty on many used vehicles, and damage has to be disclosed in some advertising. The protections have exceptions, though, and a few of them can be signed away at the dealership if you are not paying attention.

The used car lemon law: which cars it covers

New Jersey's used car lemon law regulation, N.J.A.C. 13:45A-26F, says a dealer must give the buyer a written warranty on the sale of a used motor vehicle, unless:

  1. the purchase price is less than $3,000;
  2. the vehicle is over seven model years old;
  3. it has been declared a total loss by an insurer and the buyer was told so in writing at or before the sale;
  4. it has more than 60,000 miles and the buyer chose to waive the warranty in writing; or
  5. it has more than 100,000 miles.

Exception 3 deserves attention. A dealer can sell a total-loss car without the warranty, provided it tells you in writing. If a document mentions "total loss", that is the dealer telling you about the car's history and about the warranty you are not getting.

How long the warranty lasts

The same regulation sets minimum warranty periods by mileage, whichever limit comes first:

Mileage at saleMinimum warranty
24,000 or less90 days or 3,000 miles
More than 24,000 but less than 60,00060 days or 2,000 miles
60,000 or more30 days or 1,000 miles (unless waived)

For cars over 60,000 miles, the regulation lets the buyer waive the warranty as part of a price negotiation. The waiver must be in writing, on the "As Is" Disclosure and waiver forms the regulation describes, and signed separately from the sales contract. Don't sign a separate waiver because you were told it is routine paperwork — it is a decision to give up the warranty, usually in exchange for a lower price.

Under the regulation, a refund route opens if the same material defect has been repaired three or more times, or the vehicle has been out of service a cumulative 20 or more days during the warranty period. Keep every repair order; see you bought the car and found a problem.

The lemon law doesn't apply to private sales. When you buy privately, the vehicle history is what you have — check the VIN first.

Flood vehicles and damage in advertising

The Motor Vehicle Commission has stated that the owner of a flood-damaged vehicle must place the phrase "Flood Vehicle" on the title, directly below the word "Status", and that sellers must ensure the status is disclosed to potential buyers.

The Division of Consumer Affairs also notes that it is an unlawful advertising practice for a New Jersey dealership to fail to disclose in its advertisements that a vehicle has been previously damaged when the damage exceeds $1,000. Compare the listing with what the dealer tells you in person. For the physical signs, see how to spot a flood-damaged car.

Salvage titles and restoration

The MVC describes a salvage vehicle as one wrecked, destroyed or damaged to such an extent that the insurer considers it uneconomical to repair. To return it to the road, the owner makes repairs after the salvage title is issued and then schedules an inspection; the MVC requires photographs of each side of the vehicle taken before repairs begin.

Those photos are worth asking for if you are buying a restored salvage car — they show what the damage looked like before the repair. For costs, see what a salvage title actually costs you.

New Jersey checklist

  1. Check whether the car falls inside the lemon law's coverage before relying on it.
  2. Read every document for "total loss" and for a separate warranty waiver.
  3. Look for "Flood Vehicle" under "Status" on the title.
  4. For a restored salvage car, ask for the pre-repair photos.
  5. Run a VIN check and get an independent inspection.
