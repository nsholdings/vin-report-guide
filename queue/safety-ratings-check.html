Checking a used car's crash-test ratings before you buy

Last checked November 22, 2026

Star ratings are the easiest safety information to find and among the easiest to misread. NHTSA is specific about which comparisons its ratings support, and two of its limits rule out the comparisons buyers most often want to make.

What is tested

NHTSA conducts frontal, side barrier, side pole and rollover tests, because those crash types account for most crashes. The parameters, as stated: the frontal test is head-on into a fixed barrier at 35 mph; the side barrier test uses a 3,015 lb moving barrier at 38.5 mph; the side pole test pulls the vehicle sideways at 20 mph into a pole; and the rollover rating combines the Static Stability Factor measured in the lab with a driving manoeuvre (NHTSA).

"One star is the lowest rating; five stars is the highest." The Overall Vehicle Score combines frontal and side injury probabilities with a projected injury probability from rollover resistance, and "The result is then compared to the average risk of injury across the vehicle fleet."

Limit one: you cannot compare across classes

NHTSA states that "Frontal crash rating results can only be compared to other vehicles in the same class" and whose weight is "plus or minus 250 pounds of the vehicle being rated," and that "Overall Vehicle Scores also can only be compared to other vehicles in the same class."

Side and rollover are different: "Side crash rating results can be compared across all classes" and "Rollover ratings can also be compared across all classes."

So a five-star small car and a five-star large SUV did not pass the same bar on the frontal test or the overall score. NHTSA's advertising guidance says the same thing from the other direction: comparisons "should compare only vehicles that are within 250 pounds of each other and within the same body style" (NHTSA).

A safe design and a damaged example are different things. Check the car's own history too.

Limit two: the 2011 model year break

NHTSA toughened the programme for the 2011 model year. The Federal Register rule states that "The enhancements to NCAP took effect in the 2011 model year" (Federal Register, Jul 29, 2011), and the then-Administrator's remarks put the consequence bluntly: "Beginning with the 2011 model year, earning a 5-Star Safety Ratings became that much tougher," "Safety ratings for a model year 2010 vehicle cannot be compared to those of a model year 2011 vehicle," and "never compare star ratings from the old and new systems to each other" (NHTSA, PDF).

That is directly relevant to used shopping, where a 2009 and a 2012 of the same model routinely sit side by side on the same lot. Their star counts are not the same measurement.

The upgrade added a side pole test, a small female crash-test dummy, the combined Overall Vehicle Score, and a rating for recommended advanced technologies (NHTSA final decision notice).

Where the label wording comes fromThe 2011 rule requires the overall vehicle score and frontal crash areas of the window sticker to carry "Should only be compared to other vehicles of similar size and weight." Side and rollover ratings do not require that language — which is the same split as above. See what the original window sticker shows.

The safety-concern symbol

NHTSA notes that "The symbol ⚠ alerts consumers to a safety concern the government has about the vehicle," and that "safety concerns are not part of the calculation for an Overall Vehicle Score." A car can carry a good score and a flag at the same time, so read both.

Driver assistance features

NHTSA currently flags Forward Collision Warning, Lane Departure Warning, Crash Imminent Braking and Dynamic Brake Support as features that "have met NHTSA performance tests." On a used car, confirm the car in front of you actually has them — they were optional on many model lines, and the window sticker is the document that settles what was fitted at the factory.

Recalls are the other half

Ratings describe how the model performed when tested. Whether this particular car has outstanding safety work is a separate, VIN-level question. NHTSA's VIN search at NHTSA.gov/recalls returns unrepaired recalls, and the agency lists what is excluded: "Safety recalls that are more than 15 years old (except where a manufacturer offers more coverage)," recalls already repaired, some newly announced recalls where not all VINs are identified, recalls from very small or ultra-luxury manufacturers, non-safety customer-service campaigns, and international-vehicle recalls (NHTSA). A clean result reads "0 unrepaired recalls associated with this VIN."

That 15-year exclusion matters on older cars. See open recalls on used cars and, for one component with its own history, checking a used car's airbag history.

How to look a vehicle up

  1. Search ratings at NHTSA.gov/ratings, by year, make and model — you can also search by manufacturer, and NHTSA offers a vehicle comparison tool.
  2. Compare only within class and within ±250 lb for frontal and overall scores.
  3. Do not compare a pre-2011 rating to a 2011-or-later one.
  4. Check the VIN for open recalls separately.
  5. Then inspect the individual car. A pre-purchase inspection is what tells you whether this example still performs like the one that was tested.
