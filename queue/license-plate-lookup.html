What a license plate lookup can legally tell a used-car buyer

Last checked November 22, 2026

Plenty of websites offer to tell you who owns a car from its license plate. Before you rely on one, it is worth knowing what federal law actually permits — because the restriction applies to the person obtaining the information, not only to the DMV releasing it.

The law in question

The Driver's Privacy Protection Act (DPPA) sits at 18 U.S.C. 2721–2725. It defines a "motor vehicle record" as any record pertaining to a driver's permit, motor vehicle title, motor vehicle registration or state-issued identification card (18 U.S.C. 2725).

"Personal information" in that record means information identifying an individual, "including an individual's photograph, social security number, driver identification number, name, address (but not the 5-digit zip code), telephone number, and medical or disability information." The same definition expressly says it "does not include information on vehicular accidents, driving violations, and driver's status."

The general prohibition

A state DMV and its officers, employees and contractors "shall not knowingly disclose or otherwise make available to any person or entity" personal information from a motor vehicle record, except under one of the permissible uses listed in the statute (18 U.S.C. 2721(a)).

The part people miss: the restriction also reaches private individuals. It is unlawful for a person "knowingly to obtain or disclose personal information, from a motor vehicle record" for a use not permitted by the statute, and separately unlawful to make a false representation in order to obtain it (18 U.S.C. 2722).

The VIN, not the plate, is the lawful route into a vehicle's recorded history.

Where a private car buyer fits

Section 2721(b) lists fourteen permissible uses. They cover government agencies and law enforcement; motor vehicle safety, theft, emissions and recall matters; a legitimate business verifying information an individual submitted to it; court and arbitral proceedings; research producing non-published statistical reports; insurers handling claims, antifraud, rating and underwriting; notifying owners of towed or impounded vehicles; licensed private investigative agencies acting for a permitted purpose; employers verifying commercial driver's licence information; private toll facilities; uses where the state obtained the person's express consent; bulk marketing with express consent; a requester who demonstrates written consent of the individual; and any other use specifically authorized by state law relating to vehicle operation or public safety.

Read that list as a private buyer and the result is blunt: none of the first ten describe you. The routes genuinely open are the consent-based ones — express consent obtained by the state, or written consent you can demonstrate — and whatever a specific state law may authorize. The "legitimate business" route is limited to a business verifying data the individual submitted to it, which is not a private purchase.

The penalty is specificA person who knowingly obtains, discloses or uses personal information for an impermissible purpose is liable to the individual the information pertains to. A court may award actual damages but not less than "liquidated damages in the amount of $2,500," plus punitive damages on proof of willful or reckless disregard of the law, attorneys' fees and costs (18 U.S.C. 2724).

What the statute does not say

The definition of personal information does not list the VIN, the plate number, or the make, model and year. The statute is silent on whether a plate number by itself is protected, and it says nothing about commercial plate-lookup websites or what they may display. We are not going to fill that silence with a guess, and neither should a site that wants your money. If you need a legal opinion about a specific service, that is a question for a lawyer — this page is information, not legal advice.

What to use instead

Almost everything a buyer actually needs is keyed to the VIN, not the plate, and is about the vehicle rather than the person:

  • Title brands, odometer readings, total-loss and salvage history — vehicle facts, available through VIN-based checks.
  • Open safety recalls — a free VIN search at NHTSA.
  • Whether the seller's identity matches the title — you verify that in person, against the document and their ID, not through a database.
