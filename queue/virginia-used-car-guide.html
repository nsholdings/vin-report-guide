Buying a used car in Virginia: late model, salvage and rebuilt

Last checked November 1, 2026

Virginia's salvage law is written around value rather than one headline percentage, and it contains a trap that catches buyers in other states too: a car can carry the rebuilt brand without ever having been declared salvage.

"Late model" does the heavy lifting

Most of Virginia's definitions only bite on a late model vehicle, and the term is broader than it sounds. It means the current-year model and the five preceding model years — or any vehicle whose actual cash value was at least $10,000 before it was damaged (Code of Virginia § 46.2-1600).

So an eight-year-old car still inside that value figure is a late model vehicle for these purposes. Age alone does not put a car outside Virginia's salvage rules.

The three categories

CategoryVirginia's test
Salvage vehicleA late model vehicle acquired by an insurer through the claims process (other than a stolen vehicle), or damaged so that the estimated repair cost — excluding towing, storage, rental and diminished-value payments — would exceed its actual cash value less its salvage value. A recovered stolen vehicle acquired by an insurer counts where repair cost exceeds 75% of actual cash value.
Nonrepairable vehicleDetermined by the insurer or owner to have no value except as parts and scrap metal, or for which a nonrepairable certificate has been issued or applied for.
Rebuilt vehicleAny salvage vehicle repaired for use on public highways — or any late model vehicle repaired where the estimated repair cost exceeded 75% of actual cash value, excluding the cost of repairing the engine, transmission or drive axle assembly.
The second half of "rebuilt vehicle"A late model car can meet Virginia's rebuilt definition without an insurer ever declaring it salvage — the test is the repair cost against value, with drivetrain repairs carved out. The carve-out matters: a car with a wrecked body and an untouched engine reaches the threshold faster than the repair bill suggests.

Virginia's thresholds are value-based. A full title history shows whether a brand was applied here or in another state.

What the seller owes you in writing

Virginia prohibits selling a rebuilt vehicle "without first having formally disclosed in writing that the vehicle was rebuilt". The disclosure is made on a Rebuilt Vehicle Disclosure Statement, form VSA 59 (Virginia DMV).

Ask for it before money changes hands, and keep your copy. If a brand later turns out to have been concealed, the absence of that form is the heart of the problem.

Who has to hold what

Citizens, insurance companies, auto recyclers, salvage pools and vehicle removal operators must each hold a salvage certificate, a nonrepairable certificate or a certificate of title for every salvage or nonrepairable vehicle in their possession. Licensees that demolish a vehicle or strip it for parts must also notify the DMV.

In practice this means a legitimate Virginia salvage chain leaves paperwork at every step. A seller who cannot produce the certificate for a car that plainly has been rebuilt is a seller with a gap in that chain.

What to do before you buy in Virginia

  1. Establish whether the car is a late model vehicle under either limb — age or pre-damage value.
  2. Ask directly whether a VSA 59 disclosure exists, and read it.
  3. Look for body repair with an untouched drivetrain, which is the shape the rebuilt definition is written around.
  4. Pull the title history across states — Virginia's brand does not help if the car was washed clean elsewhere. See title brands and title washing.
  5. Inspect it independently — see the pre-purchase inspection and signs of structural damage.

Confirm whether a salvage or rebuilt record exists anywhere in the car's history before you negotiate.
