Buying a used car in Georgia: salvage and rebuilt titles, older cars and emissions

Last checked October 4, 2026

Georgia's rules on damaged and older vehicles have a few features a buyer won't find everywhere: brands from other states feed into Georgia's salvage definition, rebuilt cars are inspected before they are repaired as well as after, and the oldest vehicles may not have a title at all.

What counts as salvage in Georgia

The Georgia Department of Revenue lists the conditions that make a vehicle a salvage vehicle. They include a vehicle damaged to the point that restoring it would require replacing two or more component parts; a vehicle on which an insurer paid a total loss claim and that has not been repaired, regardless of how much damage there was; and a vehicle titled in another state and branded "Total Loss", "Salvage", "Fire", "Flood" or "Water". The DOR states that salvage vehicles cannot legally be operated on public roads in Georgia.

The out-of-state point matters. A car with a flood, fire or water brand from another state is treated as salvage in Georgia and has to go through Georgia's rebuilt process before it can be driven legally. See fire-damaged cars and flood-damaged cars.

Georgia's rebuilt process: inspected before the repair

The DOR's page on titles for rebuilt or restored vehicles sets out the steps:

  • Anyone who buys a salvage or wrecked vehicle to restore or rebuild it must be licensed as a rebuilder.
  • The vehicle must be inspected before it is painted and before any repairs are made.
  • Inspections are carried out by state inspectors employed by the Department or by approved private inspectors.
  • The application includes before and after photographs, a labor and parts certification (Form T-129), and parts receipts showing the purchaser and VIN.

Those requirements mean a properly rebuilt Georgia car has a documentation trail: photos of the damage, a list of parts with receipts, and a licensed rebuilder's name. Ask the seller for copies. If none exist, ask how the car got its rebuilt title. For the buying decision, see is a rebuilt title ever worth buying?

The DOR's guidance for buyers notes that once a vehicle carries a rebuilt brand, the brand remains on the title permanently; the vehicle can be registered, but the history follows it.

Brands from Georgia and other states end up in national title data. Check the VIN before you look at the car.

Older cars: titles may not exist

Georgia lists "1962 or older year model vehicles" among vehicles that do not require a title. The DOR also says that if a 1963–1985 model year vehicle was last registered in a state or country that does not issue titles, a Georgia title will not be issued.

If you are buying a classic, the paperwork may be a registration and a bill of sale rather than a title. That makes it harder to trace ownership. Match the VIN on the car to every document, and be careful with anyone who can't explain where the car came from.

Emissions testing in metro Atlanta

Georgia's official emissions program, Georgia's Clean Air Force, says testing is required for registration in 13 counties: Cherokee, Clayton, Cobb, Coweta, DeKalb, Douglas, Fayette, Forsyth, Fulton, Gwinnett, Henry, Paulding and Rockdale. For 2026 registrations, that covers gasoline cars and light trucks of model years 2002 through 2023; the covered years move each year. The program's dealer guidance says a covered vehicle sold for highway use must have a currently valid passing emissions certificate.

"As is" is the default

The Georgia consumer protection office notes that vehicles are sold "as is" unless otherwise specified in writing. If a seller promises to fix something or to stand behind the car, get it on paper before you pay. If problems appear afterwards, see you bought the car and found a problem.

Georgia checklist

  1. Check whether the car has an out-of-state flood, fire, water, salvage or total loss brand.
  2. For a rebuilt car, ask for the before/after photos, the parts certification and receipts.
  3. For a pre-1986 car, expect thinner paperwork and verify the VIN carefully.
  4. In the 13 emissions counties, confirm the car has a valid passing certificate.
  5. Get any promises in writing, then get an independent inspection.
