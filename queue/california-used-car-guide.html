Buying a used car in California: salvage, revived salvage and smog

Last checked October 4, 2026

Two California DMV documents tell you most of what you need to know about a damaged car's past: the Salvage Certificate and the Non-repairable Vehicle Certificate. A car that went through the first can come back to the road as "revived salvage". A car that received the second cannot be registered again. Knowing which one applies stops you from paying for a car you can never drive.

Salvage Certificate or Non-repairable Vehicle Certificate

According to the DMV, when an insurer declares a vehicle a total loss salvage, the DMV can issue a Salvage Certificate. If there is an insurance settlement, the insurance company is responsible for obtaining that certificate within 10 days of the settlement date. The owner only applies personally when the vehicle was self-insured and no settlement was paid.

The same DMV page lists three situations that make a vehicle non-repairable:

  • the owner declared it solely a source of parts or scrap metal;
  • it was completely stripped; or
  • it is a completely burned shell with no usable or repairable parts.

The DMV is clear about the consequence: once a Non-repairable Vehicle Certificate is issued, the vehicle cannot be titled or re-registered. If a seller describes such a car as "a project you can register later", that is not possible under the DMV's own rules.

What "revived salvage" means on a California record

The DMV defines a revived salvage vehicle as one previously reported to the DMV as a total loss that has been rebuilt and restored to operational condition. Before it can be registered again, the owner has to submit, among other things:

  • an Application for Title or Registration (REG 343);
  • proof of ownership;
  • a vehicle verification by an authorized DMV employee, or a California Highway Patrol Certificate of Inspection (CHP 97C);
  • an electronic Vehicle Safety Systems Inspection (VSSI) certificate; and
  • depending on the vehicle, a smog certification and a weight certificate.

The DMV's registration manual adds that a vehicle previously junked or salvaged, in California or another state, must be inspected by DMV or referred to the CHP before it is registered for highway use.

Registration is not a repair-quality checkPassing these steps lets the car be registered. It does not tell you how well the structural or airbag repairs were done. Ask for the repair invoices and photos, and get an independent inspection — see the pre-purchase inspection.

Whether a revived salvage car is worth buying at all depends on the price and the repairs. The trade-offs are covered in is a rebuilt title ever worth buying?

A car rebuilt in another state and brought to California may carry a brand from that state. A VIN report can show title brands recorded across states.

Smog: the seller's job in most sales

California puts the smog certificate on the seller. The DMV's smog page says that if you are selling your car, you need to give the new owner a valid smog certificate. There are exceptions on the same page:

  • For a vehicle less than 4 years old, no smog inspection is needed at transfer; instead, the new owner pays a smog transfer fee.
  • Transfers between a spouse, domestic partner, sibling, child, parent, grandparent or grandchild do not need an inspection — unless that relative bought the car for you without first titling it in their own name.

In a private sale, be wary of "you can smog it yourself after you buy it". If the car then fails, the cost of repairs falls on you rather than the seller. For what an emissions record can and cannot tell you, see emissions inspection history.

Can you get the car's DMV record?

Usually not directly. The DMV's online Vehicle Record Request lets a person print their own vehicle registration record; requests about someone else's vehicle go by mail on form INF 70. The DMV also states that confidential information such as an owner's address is not part of the public record.

A practical workaround: ask the seller to print their own record using the online service and show it to you. It should match the name on the title and the VIN on the car. Separately, run the VIN through a report that uses national title data to see brands from other states.

California checklist

  1. Ask directly whether the car has ever had a Salvage or Non-repairable Vehicle Certificate.
  2. If it is revived salvage, ask for the repair records and the inspection paperwork from the revival.
  3. If the paperwork says non-repairable, do not buy it to drive.
  4. Confirm who provides the smog certificate before you pay.
  5. Check the VIN for brands from other states, then get an independent inspection.
