Checking a 20-year-old car: what records still exist

Last checked November 1, 2026

Everything written about checking a used car's history assumes the records exist. For an older car, many of them stop — not because nobody kept them, but because the law stopped asking. Knowing exactly where the paper trail ends tells you what to do instead.

Odometer disclosure expires

Federal law requires a seller to disclose the mileage at each transfer, but it exempts older vehicles. A vehicle manufactured in or before the 2010 model year is exempt once it is transferred at least 10 years after January 1 of its model year. A vehicle manufactured in or after the 2011 model year is exempt once transferred at least 20 years after that date (49 CFR 580.17(a)).

The regulation gives its own examples: for transfers in calendar year 2020, model year 2010 and older vehicles are exempt; for transfers in calendar year 2031, model year 2011 and older vehicles are exempt.

What that means right nowA 2010 car sold today needs no odometer disclosure at all. A 2011 car sold today still does, and will until 2031. Two cars a single model year apart, two different paper trails.

The effect on a history report is direct. Each titled transfer is one of the places a recorded reading comes from. Once disclosure stops being required, those entries thin out, and the sequence that normally exposes a rollback gets shorter and older. There is more on reading that sequence in odometer rollback: how to check the numbers.

On an older car the recorded readings that do exist matter more, because there are fewer of them.

The VIN itself changes

The standardised 17-character VIN is not as old as people assume. The federal rule requires that VINs of two vehicles manufactured within a 60-year period beginning with the 1980 model year not be identical, and the alternative format for model year 2009 and earlier vehicles uses a year-code table that begins at 1980 (49 CFR 565).

In practice that means a car from the early 1980s onward decodes normally — see decoding a VIN position by position and the check digit. Something older carries a manufacturer-specific number of varying length and format, and both decoders and report databases may simply not handle it. A "no records found" result on a 1974 car is not a red flag; it is the expected answer.

What to use instead

  • The physical car, weighted much more heavily. With thin records, an inspection stops being a double-check and becomes the primary evidence. See the pre-purchase inspection.
  • The owner's own archive. Older cars are often kept by enthusiasts who keep every invoice. Ask to see the box. Mileage figures on old invoices were written by third parties with nothing to gain.
  • State inspection records. Where a state requires periodic inspection, those records can carry dated readings — see state inspection and emissions records.
  • The title chain. Brands do not expire with age. A salvage or flood notation from 1998 is still a notation.
  • Marque registries and clubs. For some models these track individual cars by chassis number more completely than any commercial database.

Free checks still worth running

Run them, but know their limits before you read anything into a clean result — the recall lookup has an age cut-off of its own, and the free sources each cover one slice. That is set out in what you can check for free.

How to price the uncertainty

On a modern car, a thin record is a warning sign. On a 25-year-old car, it is the normal condition, and the cars worth buying are the ones where an owner happened to keep what the law no longer required. Pay for documentation when you find it. When you do not, pay for a mechanic instead.

Title brands never expire. Check the whole title history before you rely on the car's condition alone.
