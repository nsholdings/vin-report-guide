What a VIN is, and where to find it

Last checked September 6, 2026

A VIN — vehicle identification number — is the serial number for a specific vehicle. Every record that follows the car around is filed against it: title transfers, insurance claims, recalls, auction sales, odometer readings at inspection.

On any car built for the US market since the 1981 model year, the VIN is 17 characters long. It uses letters and digits, but never the letters I, O or Q, because they are too easily confused with 1 and 0. If a number you have been given is shorter than 17 characters, or contains one of those letters, it is not a valid modern VIN.

Where the VIN appears on the car

  • Driver-side dashboard — visible through the windshield from outside, at the base of the glass.
  • Driver-side door jamb — on the sticker that also lists tire pressures and the build date.
  • Under the hood or on the frame — location varies by manufacturer.

Where it appears on paperwork

  • The title (and any lien release)
  • The registration
  • The insurance card or policy documents
  • Service and repair invoices

Check that they match

This is the single cheapest fraud check available, and people skip it. Read the VIN off the dashboard yourself, then read it off the title. Compare character by character. Do not accept a VIN typed into a text message or written on a scrap of paper — plate swaps and VIN cloning both depend on the buyer not looking.

Warning signsA dashboard VIN plate with scratched rivets or fresh adhesive, a door-jamb sticker that peels, or a seller who will not let you photograph the VIN. Any of these ends the conversation.

What the VIN does not tell you by itself

The number encodes the manufacturer, some build attributes, the model year and the assembly plant. It does not encode history. Nothing in the 17 characters tells you whether the car has been wrecked, flooded, or clocked.
